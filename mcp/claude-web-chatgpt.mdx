Claude.ai, ChatGPT, Gemini Spark and Mistral on the web can connect to plamotrack as a custom connector — without you pasting a token. Instead, they use OAuth: you're redirected to sign in at your identity provider (Google, Keycloak, Authentik, etc.) and the client receives its own access tokens. This requires OIDC mode and a public HTTPS URL.

## Requirements

Before you connect a web client, make sure all of these are in place:

- plamotrack running in OIDC mode (`AUTH_MODE=oidc`) — see [OIDC Login](/authentication/oidc)
- TLS in front of your instance, with `PUBLIC_BASE_URL` set to an `https://` address — see [VPS + Caddy](/deployment/vps-caddy) or [Cloudflare Tunnel](/deployment/cloudflare-tunnel)
- `MCP_OAUTH_SIGNING_KEY` set in `.env` (generate one with `openssl rand -hex 32`)
- Your OIDC provider client configured with a second redirect URI: `<PUBLIC_BASE_URL>/mcp/auth/callback`

## Connecting Claude.ai

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open Integrations in Claude.ai">
    In Claude.ai, go to **Settings → Integrations** (or the custom connector section, depending on your plan).
  </Step>

  <Step title="Add the connector URL">
    Add a new connector and enter your plamotrack MCP endpoint:

    ```
    https://your-instance.example/mcp/
    ```
  </Step>

  <Step title="Let Claude discover your instance">
    Claude.ai discovers your instance as an OAuth server and redirects you to sign in.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Sign in at your identity provider">
    Sign in at your identity provider — for example, Google. Only your owner account is accepted; any other account is refused before a token is issued.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Done">
    Claude.ai receives its own access token and stores it. There's nothing to paste or keep track of.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Connecting ChatGPT

Follow the same steps, starting from **Settings → Connectors** in ChatGPT, and enter the same connector URL (`https://your-instance.example/mcp/`). The sign-in flow is identical.

## Connecting Gemini Spark

In Gemini Spark, open **Connected apps**, add a custom app, and enter the same connector URL. Gemini registers itself with your instance and sends you to your identity provider to sign in.

<Note>
  Gemini sometimes stops after the sign-in step without finishing the connection. If the app shows as not connected, add it again — the second attempt goes through, and the abandoned first attempt leaves nothing behind on your instance.
</Note>

## Connecting Mistral

In Mistral's Vibe Chat (formerly Le Chat) at `chat.mistral.ai`, add an MCP connector with the same connector URL and sign in when prompted. The flow is the same as for Gemini.

## What happens after an update or a restore

A connected client stays connected across a plamotrack update, a restart, and a restore from backup taken with the same `.env`. It refreshes its own access quietly; you are not asked to sign in again. If a client does ask you to reconnect, the usual causes are a changed `MCP_OAUTH_SIGNING_KEY` or `PUBLIC_BASE_URL` (both are part of the instance's identity), a restore from a backup taken before the client was linked, or a link you ended yourself. [Backups](/configuration/backups) lists which half of a backup keeps which links.

## What access does the connected client get?

Each connected client gets read and write access to your collection — the same as a read-and-write personal access token. It can log orders, move kits, adjust stock, and look up anything in your collection.

It cannot change instance settings, run a replace-all import, or manage access tokens. Those stay with your browser session and are never exposed to a connected client.

## Removing a connected client

To disconnect a client, remove the connector in the client itself. It revokes its token, and plamotrack ends the whole grant at once — the access token, the refresh token and, best effort, the provider's own refresh token behind it — whatever the provider is doing at the time. Revoking plamotrack's access in your identity provider's settings instead (its connected apps or authorised clients page) has the same effect once the client's token next comes up for refresh, as does a refresh the provider answers with a different identity.

To revoke all OAuth-linked MCP clients at once — for example, if you're changing identity providers — use the **rebind-oidc** recovery command described under [OIDC Login](/authentication/oidc#lost-access-to-your-provider-account). This clears every MCP grant and, best effort, revokes the provider's refresh tokens behind them. Your collection data, browser session, and personal access tokens are untouched. Any client that was linked can sign in again once you've reclaimed the instance.

<Note>
  Personal access tokens still work in OIDC mode. If you also use Claude Desktop with a token, it keeps working exactly as before — you don't need to change its configuration.
</Note>

<Note>
  Other AI assistants that support MCP OAuth should work the same way — add the connector URL `https://your-instance.example/mcp/` in their settings and sign in when prompted. MCP Inspector, for example, connects with its **Streamable HTTP** transport and the same URL.
</Note>

## Registration limits and edge cases

Any client can register itself with your instance in this mode, so a registration is bounded. None of the bounds touch a client that has completed its sign-in.

- Clients that register themselves are registered as **public** clients — PKCE, no client secret — whatever they ask for, and the registration response says so. A secret would add nothing when anyone can register.
- A registration lives **24 hours unless a sign-in links it**. A client that registers and never finishes signing in is forgotten; one that does is kept for as long as it is linked.
- One address may register **twenty clients an hour** (`429` past that); the instance keeps at most **1024 live registrations** (`503` with `Retry-After` past that, until unlinked ones expire); and a request body to any of the OAuth routes is capped at **16 KiB**.

Claude.ai and ChatGPT do not register at all. They bring a client description document of their own, which the instance fetches when the client shows up and keeps in memory for as long as the document's own cache headers allow — an hour when they say nothing — before fetching it again. A flood of registrations therefore cannot lock those clients out. The flip side: while such a client's document cannot be fetched — its host unreachable from your instance, and no fresh copy in memory, after a restart for example — that client's own token refresh and revocation are refused with `401 invalid_client`. Nothing is lost by it: the client's access token keeps working, the instance still refreshes the grant with your provider behind that token, and the same refresh succeeds once the document is reachable again. An instance updated from 0.3.x or 0.4.0, or restored from a backup taken on one, may hold a stored copy of such a document from before; it is used as the fallback until the first successful fetch and removed then — nothing to do.
